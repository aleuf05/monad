Abstract

Question. For a rocket with a fixed total chemical energy budget (not a fixed exhaust velocity), what schedule of exhaust velocity $v_e(m)$, as a function of remaining spacecraft mass $m$, maximizes total $\Delta v$ — and how much does that theoretical optimum actually buy you over an ordinary, constant-$v_e$ chemical rocket once real engine efficiency and energy-storage constraints are accounted for?

What this notebook derives. A closed-form optimal exhaust-velocity schedule $v_e(m) = k/m$ under a fixed-energy constraint (cells above); its closed-form $\Delta v$ compared against the classical constant-$v_e$ case; the energy that must be stored in the remaining propellant mass at each point along an optimal-schedule burn, including a worst-case figure of roughly 12–13 MJ/kg; and how adding free inert mass (in principle, from outside the rocket's own fuel budget) affects each schedule differently.

What it does not claim. This is not a proposal for a buildable engine. The optimal-schedule result is an exact consequence of the stated mathematical constraint; whether any mechanism can actually vary exhaust velocity continuously along $k/m$ and supply energy on that schedule is a separate, open engineering question, addressed directly in the Energy-Density Analysis and Limitations sections below.

Key results

  • Optimal exhaust-velocity schedule. For a fixed energy budget, the $\Delta v$-maximizing schedule is $v_e(m)=k/m$; derived rigorously above from a Cauchy–Schwarz / inner-product argument (and reproducible from the "simpler view" center-of-mass argument too).
  • Magnitude of the advantage, this vehicle. For the Saturn V S-II stage's real mass ratio, optimal-schedule $\Delta v$ (10,092 m/s) beats constant-$v_e$ at the same energy budget (9,072 m/s) by about 11% — and that ratio is independent of engine efficiency, since both figures scale identically with $\sqrt{R_e}$.
  • Free extra mass helps one schedule and hurts the other. Adding inert mass outside the fuel budget monotonically increases optimal-schedule $\Delta v$ toward a finite ceiling, but monotonically decreases constant-$v_e$ $\Delta v$ toward zero — verified both analytically (asymptotics) and numerically above.
  • The ~12 MJ/kg energy-storage requirement is not an outlandish magnitude (about 81% of the propellant's own combustion energy density) but is implausible specifically as ordinary sensible heat in the remaining propellant mass (implied ~12,700 K). Whether some other storage mechanism could deliver it is open, not resolved.
  • Compared to reality: this notebook's idealized figures exceed the actual achieved Saturn V S-II delta-v (4238.7 m/s); the gap is attributable to real-mission effects (gravity losses, finite burn, staging, the stage-mass-as-propellant simplification) that this notebook does not attempt to quantify.

Rocket equations

So, the well known rocket equation, relating the $\Delta v$ induced by a rocket burn as a function of the exhaust velocity, $v_e$, and the initial and final masses of the rocket (including any remaining fuel and payload), $m_f$ and $m_i$, comes from observing that

$$\Delta v = \int_{m_f}^{m_i} \frac{v_e}{m} dm = v_e \ln{\frac{m_i}{m_f}} $$

Energy density of fuel

This means that for fixed $v_e$, the initial mass of a rocket grows exponentially with the $\Delta v$ desired. Which is an undesirable state of things.

A natural inclination is to try to get around this by increasing $v_e$.

But that has limitations as well.

Every imaginable fuel (even matter-antimatter) has some ratio of energy stored per unit mass. Call this $R_e$

Once the energy is extracted from a piece of fuel, the remaining mass of the burnt fuel has little use except to shoot it out the back of the rocket as reaction mass (or, worse, carry along with the rocket as dead weight).

This gives us, at least for non-relativistic exhaust speeds, and negligible loss of mass through conversion to energy

$$ R_e (m_i - m_f) = \int_{m_f}^{m_i} \frac{1}{2} v_e^2 dm $$

Tying it together

This is all well and good, and gives us an upper bound for $v_e$ for chemical rockets that have a constant exhaust velocity. You can work it all out, tie it together, and figure out why constant $v_e$ chemical rockets have to be giant (and mostly consist of fuel) to achieve spaceflight $\Delta v$

But one can do more interesting things with these equations.

What if

What if we could independently schedule the release of spent fuel mass and spent fuel energy? What if we could store part of the energy from burning a piece of fuel, and use it, later, to send a different piece of exhaust at a higher $v_e$ when it is more optimal to do so? Conversely, what if we could save some of the spent fuel exhaust from burning a piece of fuel, impart all the energy onto a smaller piece of fuel exhaust, and launch that reaction mass later?

In short, what if we could make $v_e(m)$ a function of the mass remaining ($m$) ?

Those equations then become

$$ R_e (m_i - m_f) = \int_{m_f}^{m_i} \frac{1}{2} v_e^2(m) dm $$

which, if you notice, for fixed $m_i$, $m_f$ and $R_e$, constrain the inner product of $v_e(m)$ with itself.

And, subject to that, you, ideally, want to maximize

$$\Delta v = \int_{m_f}^{m_i} \frac{v_e(m)}{m} dm $$

But that's just the inner product of $v_e(m)$ with $\frac{1}{m}$ !

In short, the optimal schedule for $v_e$ as a function of the mass remaining, $m$, is to make $v_e$ proportional to the reciprocal of the total spacecraft remaining mass.

Let's solve it!!!

Start with the first equation,

$$ R_e (m_i - m_f) = \int_{m_f}^{m_i} \frac{1}{2} v_e^2(m) dm $$

And assume $v_e(m) = \frac{k}{m}$ for some unknown $k$ that we're trying to solve for

$$ R_e (m_i - m_f) = \int_{m_f}^{m_i} \frac{1}{2} \frac{k^2}{m^2} dm = \frac{k^2}{2} ( \frac{1}{m_f} - \frac{1}{m_i})$$

Or

$$k = \sqrt{ \frac{2 R_e (m_i - m_f)}{\frac{1}{m_f} - \frac{1}{m_i}}}$$

Which simplifies to $k = \sqrt{ 2 R_e m_i m_f}$

Now we want to solve

$$\Delta v = \int_{m_f}^{m_i} \frac{v_e(m)}{m} dm $$

with $v_e(m) = \frac{k}{m}$, which gets us

$$\Delta v = \int_{m_f}^{m_i} \frac{k}{m^2} dm = k ( \frac{1}{m_f} - \frac{1}{m_i})$$

$$\Delta v = k ( \frac{1}{m_f} - \frac{1}{m_i}) = k \frac{m_i - m_f}{m_i m_f}$$

$$ \Delta v = \sqrt{2 R_e m_i m_f} \frac{m_i - m_f}{m_i m_f} = (m_i - m_f) \sqrt{\frac{2 R_e} {m_i m_f}}$$

This looks comforting because we could multiply all the masses by, say, 10, and the $\Delta v$ would remain the same, just as it does with the classic rocket equation for constant $v_e$. Lets work that one out next

## Just for the heck of it, let's write a python function that computes delta v
## for an ideal-scheduled rocket as a function of m_f, m_i and Re

def deltav_optimal(m_i, m_f, Re, m_curr = None):
    """
    m_curr allows us to plan a burn schedule for a certain amount of fuel, and calculate velocity
    at any point along that burn trajectory
    """
    if m_curr is None:
        m_curr = m_f
    k = sqrt(2.0 * Re * m_i * m_f)
    return k * (1 / m_curr - 1/m_i)

Expression for constant exhaust velocity rocket

Let's work this out for a constant velocity exhaust rocket.

Recall from earlier that

$$ \Delta v = v_e \log{(\frac{m_i}{m_f})} $$

Now we just need to find $v_e$

We have

$$ R_e (m_i - m_f) = \int_{m_f}^{m_i} \frac{1}{2} v_e^2(m) dm $$

and, since $v_e$ is a constant, we just have

$$ R_e (m_i - m_f) = \frac{1}{2} v_e^2 (m_i - m_f) $$

or

$$ v_e = \sqrt{2 R_e} $$

That gives us

$$ \Delta v = (\sqrt{2 R_e}) \log{(\frac{m_i}{m_f})} $$

## And we can calculate that in python too

def deltav_constant(m_i, m_f, Re, m_curr = None):
    """
    m_curr allows us to plan a burn schedule for a certain amount of fuel, and calculate velocity
    at any point along that burn trajectory
    """
    if m_curr is None:
        m_curr = m_f
    v_e = sqrt(2.0 * Re)
    return v_e * log(m_i / m_curr)

Worked Example 2 — Proposed model (optimal exhaust schedule) — ▸ Captain

Definitions. As derived above: for a fixed energy budget $R_e(m_i-m_f)$, the $\Delta v$-maximizing schedule is $v_e(m) = k/m$ with $k=\sqrt{2 R_e m_i m_f}$, giving $\Delta v_{opt} = (m_i-m_f)\sqrt{2R_e/(m_i m_f)}$.

Derivation. Already carried out in full above (the Cauchy–Schwarz / inner-product argument, cells near the top of this notebook) — not repeated here.

Numerical example. Same S-II vehicle and the same $R_e$ used throughout.

Comparison with the conventional baseline. The ratio $\Delta v_{opt}/\Delta v_{const}$ depends only on the mass ratio $r=m_i/m_f$, not on $R_e$ (both formulas share the same $\sqrt{R_e}$ factor, which cancels in the ratio) — so overall engine efficiency changes the absolute $\Delta v$ figures but never the relative advantage of the optimal schedule over the constant-$v_e$ schedule for a given vehicle. This matches the $G(r)=(r-1)/(\sqrt r \ln r)$ gain function already named in this project's own reference notes.

What's merely mathematical vs. what needs a physical mechanism. The $v_e(m)=k/m$ schedule and its $\Delta v$ are exact, rigorous consequences of the stated energy constraint — pure mathematics, not an engineering claim. Two separate physical questions are not answered by this derivation: (1) whether an engine can continuously vary exhaust velocity along $k/m$ in real time, and (2) whether energy can actually be stored and released on that schedule. Question (2) is addressed directly in the Energy-Density Analysis section below; question (1) is not addressed anywhere in this notebook and is listed as open in Limitations.

Comparison to billiard style collisions

A natural question that might arise now is how this "optimal" schedule for $v_e(m)$ as a function of the mass remaining compares to a hypothetical "billiard ball" collision, in which the entire exhaust is ejected at once as a rigid body in a way that preserves total system momentum, and adds a kinetic energy of $R_e (m_i - m_f)$.

We calculate that below.

Here we let $v_r$ be the final velocity magnitude of the rocket, and abuse notation to use $v_e$ to denote the final velocity magnitude of the ejected exhaust (as if it were a rigid body)

Total energy in terms of $R_e$ and masses

$$ E = R_e (m_i - m_f) $$

Momentum balance

$$ m_f v_r = (m_i - m_f) v_e $$

Kinetic energy equals total energy

$$ \frac{1}{2} (m_i - m_f) v_e^2 + \frac{1}{2} m_f v_r^2 = R_e (m_i - m_f) $$

Gather terms by masses

$$ m_f v_r^2 = (m_i - m_f) (2 R_e - v_e^2) $$

$$ v_r = \sqrt{\frac{m_i - m_f}{m_f}} \sqrt{ 2 R_e - v_e^2} $$

which we can plug back in to the momentum balance equation for

$$ (m_i - m_f) v_e = m_f \sqrt{\frac{m_i - m_f}{m_f}} \sqrt{ 2 R_e - v_e^2} $$

$$ \sqrt{\frac{m_i - m_f}{m_f}} v_e = \sqrt{ 2 R_e - v_e^2} $$

Well, now we have to square things again to get anything done

$$ (\frac{m_i - m_f}{m_f}) v_e^2 + v_e^2 = 2 R_e $$

$$ (\frac{m_i}{m_f}) v_e^2 = 2 R_e $$

$$ v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2 R_e m_f}{m_i}} $$

$$ v_r = \frac{m_i - m_f}{m_f} \sqrt{\frac{2 R_e m_f}{m_i}} $$

$$ v_r = (m_i - m_f) \sqrt{\frac{2 R_e }{m_i m_f}} $$

which is exactly what our optimal schedule of releasing mass and energy gave us.

Well, that's both relieving, and somewhat disappointing. It ties everything together in a satisfying way, but raises the question of whether starting from the billiard style collision would have been a better way to reach this optimal schedule

We also have an expression for $v_e(m)$ in terms of the remaining mass amd $v_r(m)$. What happens if we subtract these?

Recall

$$ v_e(m) = \frac{\sqrt{ 2 R_e m_i m_f}}{m} $$

$$ v_r(m) = \sqrt{ 2 R_e m_i m_f} (\frac{1}{m} - \frac{1}{m_i} ) $$

If we subtract these we get

$$ v_e(m) - v_r(m) = \frac{\sqrt{2 R_e m_i m_f}}{m_i} = \sqrt{\frac{2 R_e m_f}{m_i}} $$

which is exactly the exhaust velocity from our rigid-body billiard collision thought experiment.

This, also, makes perfect sense.

How much energy does this require storing?

Well, the amount of energy released when the mass remaining is $m_r$ is $ R_e (m_i - m_r) $

At the same time, the amount released via exhaust is

$$ \int_{m_r}^{m_i} \frac{1}{2} v_e(m)^2 dm $$

where

$$ v_e(m) = \frac{\sqrt{ 2 R_e m_i m_f}}{m} $$

which gives us

$$ \int_{m_r}^{m_i} \frac{1}{2} \frac{2 R_e m_i m_f}{m^2} = \int_{m_r}^{m_i} \frac{R_e m_i m_f}{m^2} dm $$

or

$$ (R_e m_i m_f) ( \frac{1}{m_r} - \frac{1}{m_i}) $$

This makes the difference between energy released and energy expended

$$ R_e ( (m_i - m_r) - m_i m_f ( \frac{1}{m_r} - \frac{1}{m_i})) $$

or

$$ R_e ( (m_i - m_r) - (\frac{m_i m_f}{m_r} - m_f) = R_e (m_i + m_f - m_r - \frac{m_i m_f}{m_r}) $$

Energy-Density Analysis — the ~12,000 J/g figure, expanded — ▸ Captain

Mike's own eyeballed eaerlier plot read off "12000 huh?"; the exact worst-case point derived a few cells above (harmonic-mean mass remaining) lets that be computed precisely rather than read off a chart, and stated with the distinctions this kind of figure needs to be evaluated honestly.

Unit conversion. ~12,000 J/g = ~1.2 × 10<sup>7</sup> J/kg = 12 MJ/kg.

What this quantity actually is. It is the worst-case instantaneous stored-energy density required in the remaining propellant mass, at the single point along an optimal-schedule burn (mass remaining $m_r = \frac{2m_im_f}{m_i+m_f}$, the harmonic mean of $m_i$ and $m_f$) where the gap between energy already released and energy already expended via exhaust is largest. It is not an average over the whole burn, and it is not the propellant's own combustion energy density (though see below — those two numbers turn out to be closer than the "twice as hot as the sun" framing suggests).

Theoretical vs. stored vs. delivered vs. usable energy.

  • Theoretical: the full $R_e(m_i-m_f)$ chemical energy available from combustion, spread over the whole burn.
  • Stored: what must be held, ahead of use, in the mass remaining at the worst-case point — this is the ~12 MJ/kg figure.
  • Delivered: the fraction of stored energy actually converted into directed exhaust kinetic energy by whatever mechanism releases it.
  • Usable: delivered energy net of anything that has to be spent maintaining the storage itself (containment, cooling, structure)—not quantified in this notebook.

Efficiency parameter. Define $\eta$ as the fraction of stored energy that reaches exhaust kinetic energy. Worked Example 1 gives an independently-derived estimate: comparing the real J-2 exhaust velocity to this notebook's idealized 100%-efficient ceiling ($v_{e,ideal}=\sqrt{2R_e}$) gives $\eta_v = v_{e,J2}/v_{e,ideal} \approx 0.735$, hence $\eta_E = \eta_v^2 \approx 0.54$ (energy scales as $v_e^2$). This is a real engine's efficiency, not this notebook's storage mechanism's efficiency — the two are conceptually different — but it's the only grounded efficiency number available here, so it's used below as an illustrative, clearly-labeled benchmark rather than a measured value for the storage problem itself.

Ideal velocity-scale consequence. The full optimal-schedule $\Delta v$ (~10,092 m/s for this vehicle) is only reachable if the storage mechanism sustains the full ~12 MJ/kg peak. Any storage mechanism capped below that peak effectively caps the achievable schedule near that mass point; this notebook does not derive a general formula for partially-capped storage, and that derivation is left as future work.

Thermal implication (Mike's original framing, made precise). If this energy were stored purely as sensible heat in the remaining propellant using a specific heat near $c\approx 1\,\mathrm{J/(g\cdot K)}$ (roughly right for many candidate propellants), the implied temperature rise is $\Delta T = 12{,}732\,\mathrm{K}$ — consistent with Mike's "twice the surface of the sun" comparison (solar photosphere ≈ 5778 K).

The distinction that actually matters. In absolute terms, ~12.7 MJ/kg is not an outlandish energy density — it is about 81% of the propellant's own combustion energy density ($R_e\approx15.8$ MJ/kg used throughout this notebook), i.e. comparable to ordinary chemical energy storage, not orders of magnitude beyond it. The implausibility Mike originally flagged is specific to the mechanism proposed (dumping it as sensible heat into the same small remaining propellant mass via ordinary specific heat), not to the raw magnitude. A distinct, dedicated energy-storage medium — a second, more energy-dense chemical species, a phase-change or electrochemical store, anything not relying on ordinary $c\approx1\,\mathrm{J/(g\cdot K)}$ sensible heat in the same mass — remains an open, unquantified possibility rather than something this notebook can rule out. Flagged as unresolved, not claimed either way.

A simpler view

Before we move on to potential practical applications, here's another, and much simpler, way to look at these findings.

Let's go back to high school physics.

Suppose there are $N$ particles, with velocities $v_i$ for $i \in (1 ... N)$ and masses $m_i$ for $i \in (1 ... N)$

The center of mass of this system of particles moves with velocity $v_{cm} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N v_i m_i}{\sum_{i=1}^N m_i}$

Denote the velocity of each particle with respect to the center of mass as $v_{rel,i} = v_i - v_{cm}$

The total kinetic energy of the system is

$$ \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{2} m_i v_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{2} m_i (v_{cm} + v_{rel,i})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{2} m_i v_{cm}^2 + \sum_{i=1}^N m_i v_{rel,i} v_{cm} + \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{2} m_i v_{rel,i}^2$$

and we can pull terms out that don't depend on $i$ to get

$$ = \frac{1}{2} v_{cm}^2 \sum_{i=1}^N m_i + v_{cm} \sum_{i=1}^N m_i v_{rel,i} + \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{2} m_i v_{rel,i}^2 $$

But we know that $\sum_{i=1}^N m_i v_{rel,i} = 0$, we can define $m_{total} = \sum_{i=1}^N m_i$, and our equation becomes

$$ \frac{1}{2} m_{tot} v_{cm}^2 + \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{2} m_i v_{rel,i} ^2 $$

So the total kinetic energy of the system of particles is the sum of $\frac{1}{2} m_{tot} v_{cm}^2$ and $\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{2} m_i v_{rel,i}^2$

And the total momentum of the system is simply $m_{tot} v_{cm}$

So, what does this mean for rockets?

It means that, if a rocket imparts a total energy, $E$, into expelling exhaust of total mass $m_{tot}$, the way to maximize the total momentum of the exhaust is to minimize the $\sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{2} m_i v_{rel,i}^2$ term. The only way to do this is to set $v_{rel,i}=0$ for all $i$, in other words, to impart the same final velocity to all exhuast (in some inertial reference frame).

This is the same conclusion we reached above, but with a much simpler path of reasoning.

What else can we do with this?

This approach may allow us to easily work out the relativistic equivalent of the above equations, without resorting to attempting calculus of variations in Minkowski space.

This is potentially important, because even rockets that don't reach relativistic speeds may have relativistic exhaust, especially with various exotic deep space propulsion systems

Is free extra mass useful?

There are several mechanisms by which an existing spacecraft might encounter a chunk of mass that it can accelerate along with conventional rocket exhaust.

Scheduling how much of this mass to inject per unit fuel consumed can allow an otherwise conventional rocket to more closely approximate the exhaust schedule prescribed by this work.

Because of this, it's worth asking under which conditions it's worthwhile to pick up the extra mass.

Optimal exhuast schedule with extra mass

Recall that the $ \Delta v $ for a rocket with optimal exhaust schedule is

$$ (m_i - m_f) \sqrt{\frac{2 R_e} {m_i m_f}} $$

Suppose we add an extra $ m_{extra} $ of inert mass to the fuel.

Our new $ R_e $ becomes $ \frac{R_e (m_i - m_f)}{m_{extra} + m_i - m_f} $ and our new $ m_i $ becomes $ m_i + m_{extra} $

We can plug these in to get

$$ (m_{extra} + m_i - m_f) \sqrt{\frac{2 R_e (m_i - m_f)} {(m_{extra} + m_i - m_f) (m_i + m_{extra}) m_f}} $$

or

$$ \sqrt{\frac{2 R_e (m_i - m_f) (m_i + m_{extra} - m_f) } {(m_i + m_{extra}) m_f}} $$

which can be re-arranged to

$$ \sqrt{\frac{2 R_e (m_i - m_f) (m_i + m_{extra}) } {(m_i + m_{extra}) m_f} - \frac{2 R_e (m_i - m_f) m_f}{(m_i + m_{extra}) m_f} } $$

or

$$ \sqrt{\frac{2 R_e (m_i - m_f) } { m_f} - \frac{2 R_e (m_i - m_f)}{m_i + m_{extra}} } $$

and, with apologies for spelling out too many intermediate steps with too many terms,

$$ \sqrt{(2 R_e (m_i - m_f)) ( \frac{1 } { m_f} - \frac{1}{m_i + m_{extra}}) } $$

Since $ \frac{1}{m_i + m_{extra}} $ decreases with increasing $ m_{extra} $ , adding extra mass does always increase the final spacecraft velocity, albeit with diminishing returns, as an infinite value for $ m_{extra} $ gives a final velocity of

$$ \sqrt{(2 R_e (m_i - m_f)) ( \frac{1 } { m_f}) } $$

Relativistic analog — idealized kinematics — ▸ Captain (completed)

Scope, stated explicitly. This section works out the idealized relativistic kinematics and energy consequences of reaching a given speed — what $v$ costs in energy, and what it does to elapsed time. It is not a propulsion-system or vehicle design: no engine, no reaction mass, no mission is specified here. See Reading the comparison and Limitations below for exactly what is and isn't claimed.

The split from Mike's original note, preserved. This still splits into two very different-difficulty pieces:

  • The constant-$v_e$ case is standard textbook material (the relativistic rocket equation in rapidity form, $\operatorname{artanh}\beta = \frac{v_e}{c}\ln(m_i/m_f)$) and is not derived further here — it was already flagged as low-risk to add and nothing below depends on it.
  • The optimal-schedule case — this notebook's actual novel contribution — still does not survive the trick that makes the Newtonian version elegant. That argument rests on Cauchy–Schwarz applied to a quadratic energy functional and a linear $\Delta v$ functional; relativistic kinetic energy isn't quadratic in $v$, so the shortcut doesn't apply directly. Mike's own "simpler view" section above (the center-of-mass / minimize-relative-KE argument) remains a more promising starting point than calculus of variations in Minkowski space, exactly as he flags there — but carrying that argument through relativistically is a genuine, unstarted derivation. This pass does not attempt it and does not add a relativistic rocket optimizer.

What this pass does complete is the piece both cases need regardless of schedule: given a cruise speed $v$, what does relativity say about its energy cost and its time-dilation consequence. That's a closed, self-contained calculation, unlike the optimal-schedule generalization above.

Definitions, used consistently below:

  • $c$ — speed of light in vacuum, $299{,}792{,}458\ \mathrm{m/s}$ exactly (SI definition).
  • $\beta = v / c$ — speed as a fraction of $c$.
  • $\gamma = 1 / \sqrt{1 - \beta^2}$ — the Lorentz factor.
  • Coordinate time $t$ — elapsed time as measured by an observer at rest relative to the launch point (e.g. Earth/mission control).
  • Traveler proper time $\tau$ — elapsed time as measured by a clock riding with the moving traveler; related to coordinate time by $\tau = t / \gamma$.
  • Relativistic kinetic energy, per unit rest mass, $\mathrm{KE}_{\mathrm{rel}}/m = (\gamma - 1)c^2$, contrasted below with the Newtonian estimate $\mathrm{KE}_{\mathrm{newt}}/m = \frac{1}{2}v^2$.

## Relativistic analog -- worked comparison ▸ Captain

c = 299792458.0  # m/s, exact by SI definition

def lorentz_gamma(beta):
    return 1.0 / sqrt(1.0 - beta ** 2)

def relativistic_ke_per_kg(beta):
    # (gamma - 1) * c^2, in J/kg
    return (lorentz_gamma(beta) - 1.0) * c ** 2

def newtonian_ke_per_kg(beta):
    # 1/2 v^2, in J/kg -- the estimate you'd (wrongly) use ignoring relativity
    return 0.5 * (beta * c) ** 2

def traveler_time_factor(beta):
    # proper time elapsed per unit of coordinate/external time: tau / t = 1 / gamma
    return 1.0 / lorentz_gamma(beta)

betas = [0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99]

print(f"{'beta':>6}  {'gamma':>10}  {'KE_rel J/kg':>14}  {'KE_newt J/kg':>14}  {'KE_rel/KE_newt':>15}  {'tau/t':>8}")
for beta in betas:
    gamma = lorentz_gamma(beta)
    ke_rel = relativistic_ke_per_kg(beta)
    ke_newt = newtonian_ke_per_kg(beta)
    ratio = ke_rel / ke_newt
    tau_over_t = traveler_time_factor(beta)
    print(f"{beta:6.2f}  {gamma:10.4f}  {ke_rel:14.4e}  {ke_newt:14.4e}  {ratio:15.3f}  {tau_over_t:8.4f}")

# One simple plot: how far the Newtonian estimate diverges from the
# relativistic figure as beta approaches 1, with the four table points marked.
betas_fine = [0.001 * i for i in range(1, 991)]
ratios_fine = [relativistic_ke_per_kg(b) / newtonian_ke_per_kg(b) for b in betas_fine]
plt.plot(betas_fine, ratios_fine)
plt.scatter(betas, [relativistic_ke_per_kg(b) / newtonian_ke_per_kg(b) for b in betas], zorder=5)
plt.xlabel("beta = v / c")
plt.ylabel("KE_rel / KE_newt")
plt.title("Relativistic vs. Newtonian kinetic-energy estimate, per kg")
plt.show()

Reading the comparison — ▸ Captain

What the table shows. Even at $\beta = 0.5$ the Newtonian estimate already understates the true energy cost by a double-digit percentage; by $\beta = 0.99$ it is off by roughly an order of magnitude, and $\gamma$ is climbing toward infinity as $\beta \to 1$. The $\tau/t$ column shows the same regime shift from the traveler's side: at $\beta = 0.99$ the traveler's own clock runs at about $1/7$ of the external rate. Both columns are the same statement — the energy cost and the time-dilation payoff both accelerate together as $v \to c$ — read from two different directions.

Reaching cruise speed is only half a rendezvous mission. Everything above is a kinematic snapshot at a single constant $\beta$. A real rendezvous mission still needs, at minimum: an acceleration phase up to that cruise $\beta$, and a braking phase back down at the destination — neither is modeled here, and the outbound energy/propellant cost and the arrival (braking) energy/propellant cost must not be silently merged into one number. This section gives the cruise-speed energy and time-dilation figures only; it does not give a mission $\Delta v$ or propellant budget.

Three layers of claim, kept separate:

  1. Exact relativistic mathematics — $\gamma$, $\mathrm{KE}_{\mathrm{rel}}$, and $\tau/t$ as defined above are exact consequences of special relativity, not approximations or assumptions.
  2. Illustrative mission assumptions — the four $\beta$ values (0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99) are chosen only to span the regime where Newtonian intuition breaks down; they name no real mission, distance, or vehicle.
  3. Unresolved engineering feasibility — whether any propulsion mechanism could deliver a payload to these speeds, and brake it back down, is not addressed by (1) or (2) and is not claimed here. See Limitations immediately below.

Limitations — relativistic section — ▸ Captain

This section establishes kinematics and energy bookkeeping only. It does not address, and this pass does not attempt to resolve:

  • Propulsion mechanism — no engine or reaction-mass scheme capable of reaching these speeds is specified.
  • Conversion efficiency — no assumption about how efficiently any stored energy converts to kinetic energy of the payload.
  • Reaction mass — whether the $\mathrm{KE}_{\mathrm{rel}}$ figures above are paid for by expelling mass (and how much) is unspecified.
  • Waste heat — the ~12 MJ/kg storage-plausibility problem flagged earlier in this notebook for the non-relativistic case only gets worse here and is not re-examined.
  • Shielding and impact hazards — collisions with interstellar gas/dust at these speeds are a known hard problem for relativistic travel and are not modeled.
  • Acceleration tolerance — no g-limit (crewed or uncrewed) is assumed for how quickly $\beta$ is reached.
  • Braking — as stated above, no deceleration phase or its energy cost is modeled.
  • System and payload mass — the KE figures are per kilogram; no vehicle, structure, or payload mass is assumed or budgeted.

Conclusion. The relativistic mathematics establishes the rapidly increasing energy and time-dilation consequences of high velocity. It does not, by itself, establish a feasible propulsion architecture. Further development requires an explicit engine model, efficiency assumptions, acceleration and braking profiles, and complete system-mass accounting.

Results summary — ▸ Captain

  • Optimal exhaust-velocity schedule. For a fixed energy budget, the $\Delta v$-maximizing schedule is $v_e(m)=k/m$; derived rigorously above from a Cauchy–Schwarz / inner-product argument (and reproducible from the "simpler view" center-of-mass argument too).
  • Magnitude of the advantage, this vehicle. For the Saturn V S-II stage's real mass ratio, optimal-schedule $\Delta v$ (10,092 m/s) beats constant-$v_e$ at the same energy budget (9,072 m/s) by about 11% — and that ratio is independent of engine efficiency, since both figures scale identically with $\sqrt{R_e}$.
  • Free extra mass helps one schedule and hurts the other. Adding inert mass outside the fuel budget monotonically increases optimal-schedule $\Delta v$ toward a finite ceiling, but monotonically decreases constant-$v_e$ $\Delta v$ toward zero — verified both analytically (asymptotics) and numerically above.
  • The ~12 MJ/kg energy-storage requirement is not an outlandish magnitude (about 81% of the propellant's own combustion energy density) but is implausible specifically as ordinary sensible heat in the remaining propellant mass (implied ~12,700 K). Whether some other storage mechanism could deliver it is open, not resolved.
  • Compared to reality: this notebook's idealized figures exceed the actual achieved Saturn V S-II delta-v (4238.7 m/s); the gap is attributable to real-mission effects (gravity losses, finite burn, staging, the stage-mass-as-propellant simplification) that this notebook does not attempt to quantify.

Modeling simplifications, stated explicitly:

  1. Every worked example here treats the entire Saturn V S-II stage mass shed during the burn as propulsively-ejected reaction mass, including the stage's own dry structure jettisoned at staging.
